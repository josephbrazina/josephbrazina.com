Traffic Signal Detection Experiment

How simulated deutan color vision deficiency and shortened glance time affect a driver’s ability to tell a red signal from a green one, analyzed with signal detection theory.

Course
ISE 5604: Human Information Processing
Instructor
Dr. Charlie Klauer
Team
Joseph Brazina & Andrew Jones
Term
Fall 2026

I built the 80 stimuli and led the experimental design for the go/brake detection task.

The problem

Safe driving depends on detecting signals: each traffic light comes down to “signal present” or “signal absent.” Two factors can disrupt that. Deutan color vision deficiency (CVD) weakens or removes the eye’s green-sensing cones, so reds look more yellow and greens look more gray. Distraction shortens on-road glances and makes scanning more random.

The two may compound for drivers with CVD. We built a non-immersive driving simulation to test whether simulated deutan severity and shortened glance time reduce how accurately people respond to traffic signals.

Why it matters: data like this can inform machine learning models in automotive systems, supporting accessibility features for drivers with CVD and safety interventions for distracted drivers.

Questions and hypotheses

Research questionAlternative hypothesis
1Does more severe simulated deutan deficiency reduce correct responses?More severe deficiency reduces accuracy.
2Does a longer glance, from 200 to 516.7 ms, improve correct responses?Shorter glances reduce accuracy.

The null hypotheses: neither factor is significantly related to response accuracy.

Method

Two independent conditions each varied one factor and held the other constant. Red signals counted as signal present (Brake) and green as signal absent (Go), so every response is a hit, miss, false alarm, or correct rejection.

VariesHeld constantTrials
Condition 1Glance time, 200 to 516.7 ms50% simulated deutan deficiency40: 20 red, 20 green
Condition 2Deutan severity, 24% to 100%350 ms glance time40: 20 red, 20 green

Dependent variable: the proportion of participants who responded correctly on each trial.

  • ParticipantsSix adults aged 18 to 44, two male and four female, all with normal or corrected vision and no CVD. Five took part for course credit and one volunteered.
  • StimuliTen red and ten green real traffic-light images (Ultralytics), each used twice per condition for 80 clips, processed with the RGBlind simulator. Each clip was rendered in DaVinci Resolve at 60 fps, so one frame adds exactly 16.7 ms.
  • ProcedureAbout 10 minutes, self-paced, in PowerPoint on a MacBook Air. After four practice trials, randomized signals flashed then cut to black, and participants wrote G for Go or B for Brake. Condition order was counterbalanced.

Results

The hypotheses were partly supported. Spearman rank-order correlations, run in JASP, related each predictor to the proportion of correct responses across the 40 trials in its condition.

2Deutan severityExcluding 5b−.24.134

Glance time had no significant effect. Accuracy didn’t improve as glances lengthened from 200 to 516.7 ms, with or without Participant 5b.

Deutan severity did, with a caveat. More severe simulated deficiency was linked to lower accuracy, suggesting red and green become harder to tell apart. But Participant 5b made 18 of the 19 misses in Condition 2, and without that participant the relationship was no longer significant.

  • 91%Overall hit rate: most red signals were correctly called “Brake.”
  • 2.37–4.50Sensitivity (d′), with three participants at the 4.5 ceiling.
  • 1.2%–23%False-alarm rate, so decision criteria varied widely.
  • 18 of 19Condition 2 misses came from one participant.

Limitations and implications

  • Ceiling effectsA 91% hit rate and three participants at d′ = 4.5 suggest the task was too easy to reveal differences.
  • One influential participantRemoving Participant 5b removes the main correlation, so more data is needed before drawing conclusions.
  • Narrow rangesFuture work should use wider ranges of glance time and severity, with larger steps between levels.
  • Non-immersive setupResults may not carry over to real driving; a medium- or high-fidelity simulator would help.

The results only faintly suggest that deutan deficiency affects signal detection as it approaches deuteranopia, and they don’t replicate earlier findings on glance time. Given that prior research, neither factor should be dismissed. The wide spread in sensitivity across participants supports designing signals, signs, and automotive safety software around individual differences in color vision and distraction.
