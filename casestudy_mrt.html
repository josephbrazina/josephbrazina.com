Dual-Task Interference: A Multiple Resource Theory Experiment

A browser-based experiment comparing Tetris played alone with Tetris paired with a visual mental rotation task or an auditory phoneme task, to test which pairing costs more.

I designed the experiment, recorded and processed the auditory stimuli, selected the mental rotation stimuli, and built the interface. My partner, Andrew Jones, helped refine the hypotheses and design, such as running Tetris alone first and counterbalancing only the two dual-task conditions.

The question

When we do two things at once, how much they interfere depends partly on which mental resources each task uses. This experiment turns that prediction into three comparable, timed blocks.

Research question: Does Tetris performance drop more, and does perceived workload rise more, when Tetris is paired with visual mental rotation than when it’s paired with auditory phoneme discrimination?

This page documents the design and planned comparisons. No participant results are reported yet.

The prediction

Multiple resource theory (MRT) predicts more interference when two tasks draw on the same processing resources. Tetris needs continuous visual monitoring, spatial planning, and manual control, so the two secondary tasks overlap with it to different degrees (Wickens et al., 2022).

  • Tetris + mental rotation: more overlapBoth are visual, spatial, and manual, so this pairing should cause the larger drop in Tetris performance and the larger rise in NASA-TLX workload.
  • Tetris + phonemes: less overlap, not noneThe input is auditory and verbal rather than visual and spatial, sharing only the manual response, so it should cost less. Response selection and timing can still compete.

Recent computational MRT work found that modeling resource conflict, not just demand, improves predictions of dual-task interference, and that people may protect the harder task, leaving the easier one to absorb the cost (El Iskandarani et al., 2025).

Study design

Each participant first practices each task for 30 seconds. Then come three three-minute blocks: Tetris alone always first as the baseline, followed by the two dual-task blocks in counterbalanced order.

OrderConditionParticipant doesTests
1st, alwaysTetris alonePlays the falling-block game.The baseline for performance and workload.
2nd or 3rdTetris + mental rotationPlays while judging whether two 3D figures are the same shape.High overlap: visual, spatial, and manual.
2nd or 3rdTetris + phoneme discriminationPlays while judging whether two sounds are the same.Low overlap: only the manual response is shared.
  • Same exposureEvery test block lasts three minutes.
  • Fixed pacing36 rotation pairs at 5 seconds each, or 60 sound pairs at 3 seconds each. A missed response counts as incorrect.
  • Shared keysA means same and F means different in both judgment tasks; the arrow keys and Space control Tetris.

The two secondary tasks

  • Mental rotationBased on Shepard and Metzler (1971). Each pair shows the same figure rotated in the picture plane or in depth (0° to 180° in 20° steps), or a figure beside its mirror image. Each block draws 36 pairs, half same and half different, from 300 stimuli by Kim, Lester, and Yang (2025).
  • Phoneme discriminationBased on Liberman et al. (1957). Each trial plays two steps from a seven-step /ga/ to /ba/ continuum. Each block has 60 trials: 28 same pairs and 32 different pairs three steps apart.

I recorded the syllables myself, one /ba/ and one /ga/, then built the seven-step continuum between them in Praat with Claude’s help. Steps 2 to 6 are ambiguous blends designed to be hard to tell apart. Listen with headphones:

The build

The study runs as a single HTML page that walks each participant through consent, eligibility, optional demographics, instructions, practice, the test blocks, workload ratings, and completion. Tetris is drawn on a canvas, and the image and sound files load from a companion stimuli/ folder.

  • ControlsArrow keys move, rotate, and soft-drop pieces; Space hard-drops. A and F answer same and different.
  • WorkloadAfter every test block, participants rate six NASA-TLX dimensions from 0 to 100. The page reports an unweighted raw TLX mean, not the weighted score (NASA Ames Research Center, 1986).
  • DataFor each judgment it records the correct answer, the response, accuracy, timeouts, and response time; for Tetris, lines cleared, pieces placed, top-outs, and drop behavior.
  • PrivacyA combined CSV downloads to the participant’s own device at the end. The page uploads nothing.

Current state: the software and measurement flow are ready for piloting. Recruitment comes next, once any required approval or exemption is confirmed.

The analysis plan

The main comparisons are on Tetris, the task every condition shares. Tetris alone against each dual-task block gives each pairing’s cost, and the two costs are then compared, alongside NASA-TLX workload for each block.

Secondary-task accuracy and response time describe how participants handled each judgment, but rotation and phoneme scores aren’t compared directly, since the tasks differ in stimuli and pacing. Response time will be read alongside accuracy to catch speed–accuracy trade-offs.

Next steps

  1. Deploy. Publish the experiment with all seven sound files and the image folders, and check it in the intended browsers with headphones.
  2. Pilot. Verify audio, images, timing, keys, workload prompts, CSV fields, and the full session flow.
  3. Confirm governance. Settle any required review, finalize consent and privacy wording, and decide how CSV files will be transferred and secured.
  4. Balance the orders. Track centrally who gets rotation second and who gets phonemes second.
  5. Collect and compare. Predefine exclusions and primary outcomes, collect data, then compare paired conditions with uncertainty reported alongside any effects.

What I want to learn: whether visual-spatial overlap causes a bigger measurable cost to Tetris than a cross-modal pairing, and whether reported workload rises with it.
