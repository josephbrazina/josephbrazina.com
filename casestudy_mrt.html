What happens when two tasks compete for attention?

I built a browser-based multiple resource theory experiment to compare Tetris played alone with Tetris paired with visual mental rotation or auditory phoneme discrimination.

01 / Overview

The question behind the build

When we do two things at once, the interference depends partly on which mental resources each task draws on. This experiment asks whether adding a visual-spatial judgment to Tetris disrupts performance differently from adding an auditory sound judgment. It turns an abstract human factors prediction into five comparable, timed blocks.

Research question: How do Tetris performance, secondary-task accuracy and response time, and perceived workload change when Tetris is paired with visual mental rotation versus auditory phoneme discrimination?

This page documents the experiment design and intended comparisons. No participant results or effect sizes are reported yet.

02 / Theory

Multiple resource theory in practice

Multiple resource theory (MRT) predicts greater interference when concurrent tasks demand overlapping processing resources. Here, Tetris calls for continuous visual monitoring, spatial planning and manual control. Mental rotation adds another visual-spatial comparison; phoneme discrimination shifts the incoming information to the auditory channel. That makes the two dual-task conditions a useful test of resource overlap. Recent computational MRT work found that modeling resource conflict, and not demand alone, improved predictions of dual-task interference, with a further gain from accounting for visual separation between tasks (El Iskandarani et al., 2025).

Prediction to test, not a finding: Tetris paired with mental rotation may show a larger dual-task cost than Tetris paired with phoneme discrimination because of greater visual-spatial overlap. MRT does not imply the auditory pairing is interference-free: response selection, time pressure and motor input may still be shared. Task priority matters too: participants told to weight two tasks equally may still protect the more demanding one, leaving the easier task to absorb more of the cost (El Iskandarani et al., 2025).

03 / Study design

Five conditions, one participant

Each participant first practices Tetris, phoneme discrimination and mental rotation for one minute apiece. The test phase contains five three-minute conditions: three single-task baselines and two simultaneous dual-task blocks. The code defines ten rotated and reversed orders to counterbalance the test sequence; assignments can be entered manually across devices or rotated automatically within one browser.

01 / Fixed blocks

Same exposure

Each test condition lasts three minutes; practice lasts one minute per task.

02 / Timed trials

Same pacing

Rotation pairs change every 6 seconds; sound pairs are scheduled every 3 seconds.

03 / Same mapping

Less switching

S means same and D means different in both judgment tasks; arrow keys and Space control Tetris.

The setup calls for 30 mental-rotation pairs or 60 phoneme pairs per three-minute condition. The experiment keeps a Tetris gravity setting of 650 ms per cell; timing and browser behavior should be checked in a pilot.

04 / Stimulus examples

See the shapes. Hear the sounds.

These examples make the two secondary tasks tangible. The visual task asks participants to decide whether two three-dimensional figures show the same arrangement under rotation or a mirrored arrangement, following the mental rotation paradigm of Shepard and Metzler (1971), who found that the time to make this judgment grows linearly with the angle between the two views. The auditory task asks whether two sequential phonemes are the same or different. Same–different discrimination is a standard tool in categorical perception research, where consonants tend to be discriminated more categorically than vowels (Altmann et al., 2014), although what this paradigm reveals about underlying perception and auditory memory remains debated (Massaro & Hary, 1983; Tartter, 1982). In the actual experiment, participants respond with S or D while either working on the judgment alone or simultaneously playing Tetris.

05 / Implementation

From theory to a usable experiment

I implemented the study as a single HTML experiment interface with separate consent, eligibility, demographic, instruction, practice, test, workload and completion views. The actual tasks are interactive: a canvas-based Tetris board, image-pair mental-rotation trials and paired WAV sounds. The browser loads the image and audio files from a companion stimuli/ directory.

  • Task controls: Arrow keys move, rotate and soft-drop pieces; Space hard-drops to the ghost position. S and D answer either comparison task.
  • Workload after every test block: Six NASA-TLX dimensions are rated from 0 to 100 in steps of 5; the interface also calculates an unweighted mean. That mean is a raw TLX-style summary, not a weighted TLX score; the original procedure weights each dimension using 15 pairwise comparisons (NASA Ames Research Center, 1986).
  • Participant flow: The interface presents a consent decision, screens self-reported vision and hearing eligibility, asks optional demographic questions besides age, then walks through practice and test blocks.
  • Local export: After the final workload ratings, a combined CSV downloads on the participant's device. The page itself does not upload those data. Automatic order assignment uses browser-local storage and cannot coordinate across devices.
Current state: ready for piloting, not a results page

The software and measurement flow are in place. Recruitment and participant data collection are the next stage; approval or exemption requirements must be confirmed before recruiting or collecting human-subject data.

06 / Evaluation

What the data can answer

The exported event-level CSV distinguishes practice from test, labels each condition and task, and records trial responses, correctness, timeouts and response times. Tetris rows include placements and summary measures such as lines cleared, pieces placed, top-outs, placement time and hard-drop behavior. Each test block adds six workload ratings and an unweighted summary.

The primary comparisons should be within each task: Tetris alone versus each Tetris pairing; rotation alone versus rotation with Tetris; and phonemes alone versus phonemes with Tetris. Comparing dual-task costs against the matching single-task baseline is more informative than comparing raw phoneme accuracy with raw rotation accuracy, because those tasks have different stimuli and pacing.

Before analysis, define how to handle missing responses, top-outs and incomplete blocks, and select primary outcomes (for example, Tetris lines cleared and secondary-task accuracy). Inspect response time alongside accuracy for speed–accuracy tradeoffs; use workload ratings to describe the experience rather than treating them as proof of resource competition.

07 / Next steps

Collect data, then test the prediction

  1. Prepare deployment: publish the experiment HTML alongside all seven stimuli/step1.wav–step7.wav files and the rotation/mirror image folders on a web server; check it in the intended browsers with headphones.
  2. Pilot the session: verify audio playback, image loading, trial timing, key mapping, workload prompts, CSV fields and the full ~20–30 minute flow; ensure the page stays visible during timed blocks.
  3. Confirm study governance: verify any applicable review or approval, finalize consent and privacy wording, and decide how exported CSV files will be transferred and secured before recruitment.
  4. Assign orders across devices: coordinate the ten sequence numbers centrally when multiple participants use separate browsers; the built-in automatic allocator is only browser-local.
  5. Predefine and evaluate comparisons: specify exclusions and primary outcomes, collect participant data, then compare paired conditions and report uncertainty alongside any observed effects.

What I want to learn: Whether visual-spatial overlap produces a bigger measurable cost than cross-modal pairing—and where that cost appears: game performance, judgment accuracy, response time or reported workload.
