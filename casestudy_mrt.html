Overview

The Research Question

When we do two things at once, the interference depends partly on which mental resources each task draws on. This experiment asks whether adding a visual-spatial judgment to Tetris disrupts performance differently from adding an auditory sound judgment. It turns an abstract human factors prediction into three comparable, timed blocks.

Try the conditions yourself →

The demo runs 60-second versions of the conditions in your browser. It records nothing and is not part of the research study.

Research question: Does Tetris performance drop more, and does perceived workload rise more, when Tetris is paired with visual mental rotation than when it is paired with auditory phoneme discrimination?

My role: I designed the experiment, recorded and processed the auditory stimuli, selected the mental rotation stimuli and built the experiment interface. My partner, Andrew Jones, helped refine the hypotheses and the design, such as running Tetris alone first and counterbalancing only the two dual-task conditions.

This page documents the experiment design and intended comparisons. No participant results or effect sizes are reported yet.

Theory

Multiple Resource Theory in Practice

Multiple resource theory (MRT) predicts greater interference when concurrent tasks demand overlapping processing resources. Here, Tetris calls for continuous visual monitoring, spatial planning and manual control. Mental rotation adds another visual-spatial comparison; phoneme discrimination shifts the incoming information to the auditory channel. That makes the two dual-task conditions a useful test of resource overlap. Recent computational MRT work found that modeling resource conflict, and not demand alone, improved predictions of dual-task interference, with a further gain from accounting for visual separation between tasks (El Iskandarani et al., 2025).

Hypotheses to test, not findings: Mental rotation and Tetris share visual perception, spatial cognition and manual responses, so the Tetris-rotation pairing should produce the larger primary-task decrement: lower Tetris performance and higher NASA-TLX workload. Phoneme discrimination differs from Tetris in modality (auditory vs. visual) and processing code (verbal vs. spatial) and shares only the manual response, so the Tetris-phoneme pairing should cost less (Wickens et al., 2022). MRT does not imply the auditory pairing is interference-free: response selection, time pressure and motor input may still be shared. Task priority matters too: participants told to weight two tasks equally may still protect the more demanding one, leaving the easier task to absorb more of the cost (El Iskandarani et al., 2025).

Study Design

Three Conditions, One Participant

Each participant first practices Tetris, phoneme discrimination and mental rotation for 30 seconds apiece. The test phase contains three three-minute conditions. Tetris alone always comes first as the baseline; the two dual-task conditions follow in counterbalanced order, so half of participants get mental rotation second and half get phoneme discrimination second.

Secondary task 1: mental rotation

Based on Shepard and Metzler (1971). Each pair shows the same figure rotated in the picture plane, the same figure rotated in depth (0° to 180° in 20° steps), or two different figures. The 36 pairs per condition (18 same, 18 different) are drawn at random from 300 stimuli (150 original, 150 mirrored) from Kim, Lester, and Yang (2025).

Secondary task 2: phoneme discrimination

Based on Liberman et al. (1957). Each trial plays two steps from a seven-step /ga/–/ba/ continuum. The 60 trials per condition contain 28 “same” pairs (each step with itself, four times) and 32 “different” pairs three steps apart (1–4, 2–5, 3–6, 4–7 in both orders, four times each).

The experiment keeps a Tetris gravity setting of 650 ms per cell; timing and browser behavior should be checked in a pilot.

Stimuli

Stimulus Examples

These examples make the two secondary tasks tangible. The visual task asks participants to decide whether two three-dimensional figures show the same shape under rotation or a different shape, following the mental rotation paradigm of Shepard and Metzler (1971), who found that the time to make this judgment grows linearly with the angle between the two views. The auditory task asks whether two sequential phonemes are the same or different, following Liberman et al. (1957), a foundational categorical perception study of how listeners discriminate sounds within and across phoneme boundaries. Same–different discrimination is a standard tool in categorical perception research, where consonants tend to be discriminated more categorically than vowels (Altmann et al., 2014), although what this paradigm reveals about underlying perception and auditory memory remains debated (Massaro & Hary, 1983; Tartter, 1982). In the actual experiment, participants press A for “same” and F for “different” while simultaneously playing Tetris.

Implementation

From Theory to a Working Experiment

I implemented the study as a single HTML experiment interface with separate consent, eligibility, demographic, instruction, practice, test, workload and completion views. The actual tasks are interactive: a canvas-based Tetris board, image-pair mental-rotation trials and paired WAV sounds. The browser loads the image and audio files from a companion stimuli/ directory.

  • Task controls: Arrow keys move, rotate and soft-drop pieces; Space hard-drops to the ghost position. A answers “same” and F answers “different” in both comparison tasks.
  • Workload after every test block: Six NASA-TLX dimensions are rated from 0 to 100 in steps of 5; the interface also calculates an unweighted mean. That mean is a raw TLX-style summary, not a weighted TLX score; the original procedure weights each dimension using 15 pairwise comparisons (NASA Ames Research Center, 1986).
  • Participant flow: The interface presents a consent decision, screens self-reported vision and hearing eligibility, asks optional demographic questions besides age, then walks through practice and test blocks.
  • Local export: After the final workload ratings, a combined CSV downloads automatically on the participant's device. For each judgment trial it records the correct answer, the response, whether it was correct, whether the trial timed out and the response time in milliseconds. The page itself does not upload those data.
Current state: ready for piloting, not a results page

The software and measurement flow are in place. Recruitment and participant data collection are the next stage; approval or exemption requirements must be confirmed before recruiting or collecting human-subject data.

Evaluation

What the Data Can Answer

The exported event-level CSV distinguishes practice from test, labels each condition and task, and records trial responses, correctness, timeouts and response times. Tetris rows include placements and summary measures such as lines cleared, pieces placed, top-outs, placement time and hard-drop behavior. Each test block adds six workload ratings and an unweighted summary.

The primary comparisons are on Tetris, the task both conditions share: Tetris alone versus each dual-task condition gives each pairing’s primary-task decrement, and the two decrements are then compared, alongside NASA-TLX workload for each block. Secondary-task accuracy and response time describe how participants handled each judgment, but raw rotation and phoneme scores should not be compared directly, because the tasks differ in stimuli and pacing and neither is measured alone.

Before analysis, define how to handle missing responses, top-outs and incomplete blocks, and select primary outcomes (for example, Tetris lines cleared and secondary-task accuracy). Inspect response time alongside accuracy for speed–accuracy tradeoffs; use workload ratings to describe the experience rather than treating them as proof of resource competition.

Next Steps

Collect Data, Then Test the Prediction

  1. Prepare deployment: publish the experiment HTML alongside all seven stimuli/step1.wav–step7.wav files and the rotation/mirror image folders on a web server; check it in the intended browsers with headphones. The supplied mental rotation set has 300 images, enough for a fresh draw of 36 pairs per participant.
  2. Pilot the session: verify audio playback, image loading, trial timing, key mapping, workload prompts, CSV fields and the full session flow; ensure the page stays visible during timed blocks.
  3. Confirm study governance: verify any applicable review or approval, finalize consent and privacy wording, and decide how exported CSV files will be transferred and secured before recruitment.
  4. Balance the two orders: track centrally which participants get rotation second and which get phonemes second, so the two dual-task orders stay balanced.
  5. Predefine and evaluate comparisons: specify exclusions and primary outcomes, collect participant data, then compare paired conditions and report uncertainty alongside any observed effects.

What I want to learn: Whether visual-spatial overlap produces a bigger measurable cost to Tetris than a cross-modal pairing, and whether reported workload rises with it.
