Nintendo Switch 2 Home Screen Redesign

A wireframe and low- and high-fidelity Figma prototypes of the Switch 2 HOME Menu, with every change drawn from usability-test findings and grounded in human factors engineering (HFE) principles.

Course
ISE 5654: Human Factors System Design
Instructor
Dr. Deborah Dickerson
My role
Wireframe & prototype designer
Team
Joseph Brazina, Arsalan Ashraf & Jyotshna Bali
Date
Oct 2026

I designed the wireframe and both interactive prototypes, and wrote the report’s prototyping, HFE principles, findings, and redesign sections. My teammates led the design description and the usability testing.

The original HOME Menu

The HOME Menu is the hub players return to between games, so it has to stay legible whether the console is handheld, on a table, or docked to a TV. It’s already minimal and user-first.

The redesign keeps what the original already does well:

  • Clear cursor locationA cyan frame always shows what’s selected.
  • Button promptsCues like “Ⓐ Continue” remind players which button does what.
  • Games come firstGame cards are the most salient element, in the user’s focal vision.
  • Status at a glanceWi-Fi and battery are always visible in the top right.

What testing found, and what I changed

The team tested two participants, a novice and an expert. Each did five tasks in handheld mode while thinking aloud, then filled out the System Usability Scale (SUS). Both scored 75, but the novice struggled with unlabeled icons, while the expert wanted faster controls and more personalization.

Test findingPrototype change
Game icons should look like physical game cases, and be larger.Full portrait cover-art game cards replace the small square icons.
The title above each icon is redundant.The cover art carries the title, so there’s no separate label.
Brightness should be adjustable from the home screen.A brightness slider sits with the Wi-Fi, battery, and time in the top right.
Unlabeled icons were unclear, and the bottom icons were too small.A wider, full-width navigation bar on the bottom edge, with larger buttons closer to the thumbs.
The home screen should be customizable around favorite games.Four Nintendo-themed home screens (Mario, Zelda, Kirby, Donkey Kong) alongside Light and Dark.

Both participants were team members, so these findings are preliminary rather than generalizable.

How I built it

  1. Wireframe

    I laid out the redesign as a static grayscale wireframe on a 1920 × 1080 frame: the home screen, the theme picker, and the Mario theme applied. Arrows show the flow between screens, and eight numbered notes explain each change.

  2. Lo-fi prototype

    I added a Mario version of the home screen and theme picker, labeled every placeholder, and wired the screens together in Figma. The game row scrolls, Themes opens the picker, choosing a theme switches the home screen, and Ⓑ Back or tapping outside closes it.

  3. Hi-fi prototype

    I replaced the placeholders with real cover art (console branding and age ratings removed), Iconify nav icons, and actual Switch 2 profile icons, and added an All Software tile to end the game row. One cyan accent marks the selection frame, progress bars, and brightness slider.

    All six themes share one layout. Only the colors and background change, so I tuned each theme’s contrast separately.

Why each change works

Every change rests on an established HFE principle. Together they aim for faster game selection, easier thumb reach, fewer trips into System Settings, and a home screen players can make their own.

Design choiceHFE principlesHow it helps
Full cover-art game cardsFitts’ law; visual hierarchy; recognition over recallLarger targets are faster to tap, and familiar box art is spotted rather than read.
Wider bottom nav barFitts’ law; thumb reach on handheld touchscreensBigger buttons sit closer to the thumbs and apart from the game row.
Home-screen brightness sliderInformation access cost; functional grouping; compatible mappingA frequent adjustment no longer needs System Settings, and left dims while right brightens.
Story progress barsKnowledge in the world; proximity compatibility; redundancy gainProgress shows without opening the game, right beneath it, with a percentage as backup.
Cyan selection frame, glows, shadows, and per-theme nav colorsAttentional salience; figure-ground segregation; luminance contrastThe cursor is easy to find, and covers and buttons stay distinct on every background.
Themed home screens with image previewsPersonalization; aesthetic-usability effect; consistency; recognition over recallPlayers personalize their console, the fixed layout keeps spatial memory intact, and previews show each theme.
Cyan frame and ✓ on the current theme; Ⓑ Back or tap outsideRedundancy gain; discriminability; user control and freedomTwo cues mark the selection, and there are two quick ways back to the home screen.
All Software tileSignifiers; visibility of system statusThe grid icon signals the full library and shows where the row ends.

Note. Principles drawn from Fitts (1954), MacKenzie (1992), Bergstrom-Lehtovirta and Oulasvirta (2014), Wickens et al. (2022), Wickens and Carswell (1995), Nielsen (1994), Norman (2013), Sanders and McCormick (1993), Blom and Monk (2003), Kurosu and Kashimura (1995), Tractinsky et al. (2000), and Wagemans et al. (2012).

Trade-offs and next steps

Prototyping showed me how well designed the original already is, which made real pain points hard to find. Building interactive prototypes instead of static mockups let me see how each element would behave before implementation. Each change also has a cost worth weighing against Nintendo’s minimalist design language.

ChangeRiskPossible fix
Nintendo-themed home screensBusy backgrounds add clutter and cognitive load (Sweller, 1988).Simpler backgrounds, which also sharpen the edges of cards and buttons and keep the game row the focal point.
Theme-matched nav buttonsButtons colored like the background lose salience.Nav colors that contrast with each background.
Home-screen brightness sliderClutter for players who use auto-brightness.A setting to show or hide the slider.
Theme picker pop-upOnly six themes fit at once.A larger pop-up if more themes are added.

Next step: run a usability test on the hi-fi prototype to confirm whether each HFE principle actually improves the experience, and decide which changes to keep, adjust, or remove.
