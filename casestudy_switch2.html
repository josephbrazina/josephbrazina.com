Nintendo Switch 2 Home Screen Redesign

A redesign of the Switch 2 home screen built around full cover art, visible story progress, a full-width bottom navigation bar, and a theme system with Nintendo-themed backgrounds. It went from a low-fidelity wireframe to high-fidelity Figma prototypes.

Overview

The Problem

The home screen is the first thing every Switch 2 player sees and the hub they come back to between games. I led a three-person team through a human factors review of it. We defined target users, ran task-based usability tests to find pain points, and I designed Figma prototypes that address them.

This page shows the redesign that came out of those tests. It covers the annotated wireframe, the low- and high-fidelity interactive prototypes, and a six-option theme system that changes the whole home screen.

Redesign Goals

Four Changes to the Home Screen

01

Full cover art

Full portrait box art in a row that scrolls sideways, so games can be recognized at a glance.

02

Story progress

A 0–100% bar under each game. Games without a story mode show no bar.

03

Bottom navigation

System buttons move to a full-width bar along the bottom edge, using icon-only buttons.

04

Nintendo themes

Light, Dark, and four character backgrounds, chosen from a new Themes button.

Wireframe

A Grayscale Blueprint

Before building anything interactive, I made a static, grayscale wireframe in Figma to agree on the skeleton of the new home screen. Every change in it traces back to a pain point we found in usability testing. Each screen is a 1920×1080 frame set in Inter, the font used across all three fidelity levels.

Crossed-out portrait rectangles stand in for game covers, with a black frame on the selected game and a progress bar under each title (left off for games without a story). Ten circles along the bottom became the new navigation bar, three circles in the top left hold profile icons, and the top right has the clock, Wi-Fi, battery, and a new brightness slider. Arrows walk through the main new task: open Themes, pick a theme from a 3×2 grid of previews, and see the home screen change.

Low-Fidelity Prototype

Making the Flow Clickable

The lo-fi prototype turned the wireframe into something you can click. I duplicated the light wireframe and its theme picker, then made a red copy of both to stand in for the Mario theme. Game titles went onto each placeholder cover, theme names onto each preview, and labels such as Sleep and eShop under each nav circle.

Scrolling game row

The cover row scrolls sideways, the way a player moves through games on the console.

Themes button

Opens the theme picker over the current home screen.

Switching themes

Choosing Light or Mario in the picker shows the matching home screen.

Two ways out

The Ⓑ Back button or a click anywhere outside the picker returns you to the current home screen.

High-Fidelity Prototype

From Gray Boxes to Real Screens

Each lo-fi screen maps directly to a hi-fi Figma screen. I designed the Light home screen first, then duplicated it five times for the other themes so every theme shares the exact same structure and layout.

  • Cover art. Real covers replaced the placeholders, with Switch branding, age ratings, and other additions removed so only the art remains. An "All Software" tile with a 3×3 grid icon ends the row.
  • Navigation. Colored icons from the open-source Iconify plugin, left to right: Switch Online, GameChat, News, eShop, Album, Game Cards, Controllers, Settings, Themes, and Sleep.
  • Profiles and status. Real Switch 2 profile icons (Mario, Bowser, Donkey Kong), plus Iconify Wi-Fi and battery icons.
  • Cyan accent. Used for the progress bars, the brightness slider, and a glowing frame around the selected game.
  • Separation from the background. Covers, progress bars, and status items got drop shadows and glows, and unselected covers got a subtle light gray frame.
Theme System

Six Themes, One Layout

The picker has two system themes and four Nintendo character themes. Each background was chosen to stay recognizable as a small thumbnail in the theme picker and to work behind the full home screen.

Background Themes

Themes Applied

Every theme keeps the same layout, focus outline, and progress bars. Only the background image and the colors of the time, status icons, brightness slider, progress percentages, and nav buttons change to match. Each theme picker takes on its theme's background, and the current theme is marked with a cyan frame and a ✓. Pick a theme to see its home screen and theme picker.

Human Factors Principles

Why It Looks This Way

Every change is backed by an established human factors principle, so each design decision rests on evidence instead of taste alone.

ElementDesign decisionHuman factors rationale
Cover artFull portrait box art instead of small square tilesFitts' law: bigger targets should cut selection time, especially with touch in handheld mode. Recognition over recall: players spot the box art they already know. Size and position also make the game row the top of the visual hierarchy.
Bottom navA wider, full-width bar along the bottom edgeFitts' law and thumb reach: the buttons are bigger and closer to the thumbs in handheld mode. Moving them below the game row keeps system functions secondary to games.
ThemesFour Nintendo backgrounds alongside Light and Dark, all with the same layoutPersonalization and the aesthetic-usability effect: a console that looks better tends to feel easier to use. A consistent layout keeps users' mental model and spatial memory intact when they switch themes.
Nav icon colorsIcons keep their own colors, separate from each theme's backgroundFigure-ground segregation: the buttons stay distinct from the background art and keep their luminance contrast.
Brightness sliderAdded to the status bar next to Wi-Fi and batteryMinimizing information access cost: a frequent setting moves out of the Settings menu. It's grouped by function with the other system indicators, and left = dimmer, right = brighter matches users' mental model.
Story progressA progress bar and percentage directly under each gameKnowledge in the world, not in the head: players don't have to remember how far they got. The proximity compatibility principle puts the bar right next to its game, and the bar plus the number give redundancy gain.
Selection frameA glowing cyan frame on the selected game, with shadows, glows, and gray frames on the other coversAttentional salience and discriminability: the cursor is easy to find on any background, and the covers stand apart from the art behind them.
Theme pickerImage previews with shadows, glows, or white frames to fit each background. The current theme gets a cyan frame plus a ✓Recognition over recall: players see each theme before choosing it. The edge treatments improve luminance contrast, and the two selection cues give redundancy gain. Ⓑ Back or clicking outside the picker supports user control and freedom.
All Software tileA tile with a 3×3 grid icon at the end of the game rowThe grid icon signals the full library (another redundancy gain), and its spot at the end gives visibility of system status: the row is finite, and the rest of the library is one step away.

Trade-offs

Expected gain

Themes make the console more attractive and personal, and colored nav buttons tie the whole screen to the chosen theme.

Possible cost

Busy backgrounds like Mario and Donkey Kong may add cognitive load and compete with the covers for attention. Tinting the nav buttons to match the theme can also make each button less salient.

Results

Usability Test Results

Usability testing of the current home screen is complete, and the team is compiling the results now. A full write-up of the pain points we found, and how each one maps to the redesign, will be added here soon.

Next step: validate the redesign. A round of usability validation should test whether each principle actually improves the experience. Contrast deserves particular attention: over the Mario and Donkey Kong backgrounds, the small progress labels and translucent nav buttons are hardest to read, and a scrim or stronger contrast behind text may be needed.
